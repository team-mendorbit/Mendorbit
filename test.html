Fundamental Rights of India (Articles 12–35): Quick Revision Guide

⏱ 4 min read 📘 Constitution of India 🎯 Exam & Concept Ready

Part III of the Constitution of India (Articles 12 to 35) guarantees Fundamental Rights to protect individual liberty, equality, and dignity. Here is a crisp, point-by-point breakdown of every article with practical examples.

Introductory Articles (12–13): Scope & Enforcement

Article 12 — Definition of "State"

  • Defines who is bound to respect Fundamental Rights.
  • Includes: Union & State Governments/Legislatures, local authorities (Municipalities, Panchayats), and statutory/govt-controlled bodies (LIC, ONGC, public universities).
Example: A writ petition can be filed against a PSU bank or municipality for caste-based denial of service.

Article 13 — Laws Inconsistent with Fundamental Rights

  • Establishes the power of Judicial Review.
  • Any pre- or post-constitutional law, ordinance, or order violating a Fundamental Right is void to that extent.
Example: Supreme Court struck down Section 66A of the IT Act (Shreya Singhal v. Union of India) for violating free speech.

1. Right to Equality (Articles 14–18)

Article 14 — Equality before Law & Equal Protection of Laws

  • Equality before law: No one is above the law, regardless of rank or wealth.
  • Equal protection of laws: Equals treated equally; allows "reasonable classification" (e.g., income tax slabs).
Example: Same drunk-driving penalty for everyone, but higher income tax for billionaires is legally valid.

Article 15 — Prohibition of Discrimination

  • No State discrimination solely on religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth.
  • Equal access to public places (shops, hotels, roads, wells).
  • Clauses (3)–(6): Special provisions allowed for women, children, and backward classes.
Example: Restaurants cannot deny entry based on religion; reserved seats for women in buses are valid.

Article 16 — Equality of Opportunity in Public Employment

  • Equal opportunity for all citizens in government jobs.
  • Permits reservation for underrepresented backward classes and EWS.
Example: Any eligible citizen can apply for UPSC/State PSC regardless of religion or gender.

Article 17 — Abolition of Untouchability

  • Completely abolishes untouchability and makes it a punishable criminal offence (Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955).
Example: Blocking entry to a temple, school, or village water tap based on caste is a non-bailable offence.

Article 18 — Abolition of Titles

  • Bans hereditary/aristocratic titles (e.g., Rai Bahadur, Sir).
  • Military and academic distinctions (Dr., Prof., Major) are exempt.
Example: National awards (Bharat Ratna, Padma Shri) are honours, not titles—cannot be used as prefixes/suffixes.

2. Right to Freedom (Articles 19–22)

Article 19 — Six Fundamental Freedoms

Citizens only • Subject to reasonable restrictions (national security, public order, decency, defamation)
  • 19(1)(a): Speech & expression (press, criticizing govt, silence). Ex: Newspaper article criticizing policy.
  • 19(1)(b): Peaceful assembly without arms. Ex: Candlelight march or public meeting.
  • 19(1)(c): Form associations, unions, or co-operatives. Ex: Trade union, NGO, political party.
  • 19(1)(d): Move freely throughout India. Ex: Travelling from Bihar to Kerala without a permit.
  • 19(1)(e): Reside and settle anywhere in India. Ex: Buying/renting a home in any state.
  • 19(1)(g): Practice any profession, trade, or business. Ex: Opening a store, startup, or practicing law.

Article 20 — Protection in Respect of Conviction

  • No Ex-Post Facto Law: No retrospective crime or harsher penalty than what existed at the time.
  • No Double Jeopardy: Cannot be prosecuted and punished twice for the same offence.
  • No Self-Incrimination: Cannot be forced to testify against oneself.
Example: Forced confessions made solely to police are inadmissible in court.

Article 21 — Protection of Life and Personal Liberty

  • No deprivation of life or liberty except by a fair, just, and reasonable "procedure established by law."
  • Expanded by SC to include: Right to Privacy, clean environment, shelter, healthcare, speedy trial, and choosing a life partner.
Example: Phone tapping or accessing personal data requires a strict legal warrant (K.S. Puttaswamy case).

Article 21A — Right to Education

  • Added by the 86th Amendment (2002): Free and compulsory education for children aged 6–14 years.
Example: Free admission and textbooks in nearby govt schools under the RTE Act.

Article 22 — Protection Against Arrest and Detention

  • Punitive Detention (ordinary arrest): Right to know grounds, consult a lawyer, and be produced before a Magistrate within 24 hours (excluding travel time).

  • Preventive Detention: Cannot exceed 3 months without Advisory Board approval.

3. Right against Exploitation (Articles 23–24)

Article 23 — Human Trafficking & Forced Labour

  • Bans human trafficking, begar (unpaid forced labour), and bonded labour.
  • Exception: State may impose compulsory public service (e.g., wartime military conscription) without discrimination.
Example: Forcing unpaid work to pay off a generational debt is a serious crime.

Article 24 — Prohibition of Child Labour

  • Bans employing children below 14 years in factories, mines, or hazardous jobs.
Example: Employing a 13-year-old in a fireworks factory, brick kiln, or construction site is illegal.

4. Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28)

Article 25 — Freedom of Conscience & Religion

  • Freedom to profess, practice, and peacefully propagate religion (subject to public order, morality, health).
Example: Peaceful sharing of beliefs is protected; forcible or fraudulent conversion is not.

Article 26 — Freedom to Manage Religious Affairs

  • Collective right of religious denominations to establish institutions, manage internal affairs, and own property.
Example: Temple trusts, mosque committees, or church boards managing donations and rituals.

Article 27 — Freedom from Religious Taxes

  • State cannot levy taxes specifically to promote or maintain any particular religion.
Example: No "religious tax" for monuments (administrative fees for pilgrimage crowd management are allowed).

Article 28 — Religious Instruction in Educational Institutions

  • Wholly State-maintained: No religious instruction allowed.
  • State-aided/recognized: Allowed only voluntarily with student/guardian consent.
Example: Govt schools cannot compel students to attend scripture classes.

5. Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29–30)

Article 29 — Protection of Interests of Minorities

  • Any citizen group with a distinct language, script, or culture has the right to conserve it.
  • No denial of admission to State-aided institutions solely on religion, race, caste, or language.
Example: Preserving Maithili, Bodo, or Konkani script, literature, and festivals.

Article 30 — Minority Educational Institutions

  • Religious and linguistic minorities can set up and run their own schools/colleges without discrimination in State financial aid.
Example: St. Stephen's College (Delhi), Jamia Millia Islamia.
Note on Article 31: Right to Property was repealed as a Fundamental Right by the 44th Amendment (1978) and moved to Article 300A as a legal/constitutional right. Articles 31A, 31B, and 31C are saving clauses protecting land reforms and certain Directive Principles.

6. Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32)

Article 32 — Enforcement of Fundamental Rights

  • Called the "Heart and Soul of the Constitution" by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar.
  • Direct right to move the Supreme Court (High Courts have similar power under Article 226).
  • 5 Writs: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto.
Example: Filing a Habeas Corpus petition for immediate release from illegal police detention.

Concluding Articles (33–35): Exceptions & Parliamentary Power

Article 33 — Armed Forces & Police

  • Parliament can restrict/modify Fundamental Rights of Armed Forces, Police, and Intelligence personnel for discipline and proper duty.

Article 34 — Martial Law

  • Allows restrictions on Fundamental Rights during Martial Law and lets Parliament indemnify officers for actions taken.
Example: Suspending assembly or movement during temporary military rule in a disturbed area.

Article 35 — Exclusive Power of Parliament

  • Only Parliament (not State Legislatures) can prescribe punishments for Part III offences (e.g., Art. 17, Art. 23).
Example: Only Parliament can amend the Protection of Civil Rights Act for uniform punishment across India.
